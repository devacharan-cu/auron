North of the city the plateau folds gently toward its old water tanks. Rainfold places its homes on that slope rather than flattening it. Pale terraces of lime plaster and granite step down each side, and every home looks across the valley.

Auron works one site at a time, and begins with the ground: how it slopes, where the water goes, and where the sun reaches. The drawings on this page carry the facts. The land carries the rest.

Auron and Rainfold are fictional, made for a design portfolio. Nothing here is an offer.

02Ground

Red earth, granite and held water.

The plateau around Bengaluru is a landscape of weathered granite hills and tanks (kere), linked by shallow valleys that carry the monsoon run-off from one to the next. Rainfold sits in one of those folds.

  1. i

    A shallow fold

    The valley falls about 11 m from rim to floor over 60 m of slope: gentle enough to walk, steep enough that every terrace sees over the one below.

  2. ii

    Homes on the contour

    Four long terraces follow the slope instead of a flattened platform. The earth they cut away is retained behind the homes, not carted off the site.

  3. iii

    A garden that holds water

    Rain from roofs and slopes gathers in a chain of shallow pools on the valley floor. In June they fill. In the dry months they're a garden of reeds and stones, and a walk under rain trees.

03Section A–ALooking north

Move the sun across the drawing.

A section is the architect's way of cutting through a building to show how it meets the ground and the sky. This one is live. Pick a time and a day of the year to see where the sun lands in each home, and where the eaves, louvres and trees hold it back.

  • Cut through earth and structure
  • Seen beyond
  • Direct sun on floors, ground and roofs
  • Shade

How the light is drawn. The sun's position is calculated with standard declination and hour-angle formulas for 13.1° N, in Indian Standard Time. Shadows are traced through this drawing's own geometry. The section treats the terraces as long and running north–south, so it shows only the east–west part of the sunlight. The plans use the sun's full direction. Terrain and buildings are invented, so the result is a concept visualisation, not a daylight study or a prediction for a real building.
