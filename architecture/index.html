ArchitectureDesign philosophy

Cut into the slope, open to the light.

  1. 01

    Follow the ground.

    The terraces step with the slope. Earth cut from behind each one is held by its back wall, so the valley keeps its shape and nothing becomes a podium.

  2. 02

    Open both ways.

    Every home looks across the valley at the front and onto a courtyard at the back. Air crosses it, and bedrooms take the quieter side.

  3. 03

    Hold the light back where it is hardest.

    Deep eaves keep the high midday sun off the glass. Timber louvres take the low afternoon sun on the west-facing side. The morning sun is let in.

The section, live.

The whole site in one cut, looking north. Pick a time and a day of the year. The clock is shared with every other drawing on this site, so the homes you look at next are shown in the same light.

How the light is drawn. Standard declination and hour-angle formulas for 13.1° N in Indian Standard Time, with shadows traced through this drawing's own geometry. The section shows only the east–west part of the sunlight; plans use its full direction. Terrain and buildings are invented: a concept visualisation, not a daylight study.

  1. 1

    Planted roof. Soil over the top slab, looked down on from the terrace above, so it reads as a garden rather than a roof.

  2. 2

    Eave. The slab runs 0.6 m past the verandah edge here, and 0.9 m on the east-facing terrace, where only the high sun must be stopped.

  3. 3

    Timber louvres. On west-facing verandahs only. They break the low afternoon sun before it reaches the living room.

  4. 4

    Verandah. 3.4 m deep and the width of the home: a room outside, between the louvres and the glass.

  5. 5

    Back windows. Above the retained earth, onto the courtyard: the second side of every home.
