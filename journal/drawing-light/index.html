Drawing light at 13° north

How the living section calculates its sun, and why June is the strange month.

Most property sites draw the sun the way children do: a disc in a corner, the same at every hour. The section on this site moves its sun with a small piece of astronomy, because the point of the drawing is to answer a buyer's real question: when does light reach my living room?

Three numbers

The sun's position depends on three things. The latitude: the drawing assumes 13.1° N, roughly the north of Bengaluru. The day of the year, which sets the sun's declination, how far north or south of the equator it stands at noon. And the time, which becomes an hour angle once Indian Standard Time is corrected for longitude and for the equation of time, the small drift between clock noon and solar noon over the year.

From those, standard formulas give the sun's altitude above the horizon and its compass bearing. The drawing uses textbook approximations good to a degree or two. That is enough to show direction and season honestly. It isn't enough for an engineer, and it isn't meant to be.

Why June is strange

Bengaluru lies south of the Tropic of Cancer. Between May and July the noon sun at 13° N passes slightly north of overhead, so for a few weeks north-facing walls get direct sun at midday and shadows on the plan point south. In December the noon sun is about 54° up, in the south. Few people who grew up further north expect this, and it changes which rooms are bright in which season.

What the section can and cannot show

A section is a cut. Treating the terraces as long and running north–south lets the drawing cast shadows using only the east–west part of the sunlight, which is why it is so clear about mornings and afternoons. The north–south part, the June effect, shows on the plans instead. Terrain, buildings and trees are all invented, so the hours of sun the site reports for each home are consistent with its drawings and with nothing else. A real project would commission a daylight study.
